1. abstract

This paper brings theorizing from Trans Studies to bear on current methods for detecting and mitigating anti-trans bias in LLM-generated text. It looks at common frameworks of evaluating gender and anti-trans bias in LLMs and weighs these in light of recent theorizing in Trans Studies. In particular, Kadji Amin’s framework of gender identification, which promotes a definition for gender categories based on visible and expressive qualities. The survey of current tools is followed by a discussion on implications of marking gender as identity or as expression. It then reviews some recent work in evaluating gender bias that makes some progress in this area, and makes suggestions for future research.

2. introduction

There is a global urgency to understand and conceptualize gender, how it ought to be defined and legislated.

This paper argues that one particular difficulty of mitigating gender bias in NLP has to do with the general disagreement about what gender is in the first place.

This paper considers current conversations in Trans Studies that pursue a conceptualization of gender that pushes against this ambiguity. It then explores how these conceptualizations might be applied to current methods in NLP.

3. Gender ideology

What Judith Butler refers to as the "phantasm" of "gender ideology" is a world-wide phenomenon (Butler 2023). The term "gender ideology," which the Southern Poverty Law Center classifies as derogatory, refers to the conservative and far-right view that LGBTQ+ identity is an internal and false belief system (SPLC 2023).

In various countries around the world, gender ideology has become a national issue, serving as a rallying cry for various political groups togarner support (Butler 2023). For example, in the European continent, far-right groups have used transphobia to prop up other fears, such as those against migrants, in what (Indelicato and Lopes, 2024) argue is part of a larger white supremacist campaign. In the African continent, the same fear has been deployed in anti-colonial discourses, which assert that gender ideology is a dangerous import from the West which threatens traditional gender roles and the nuclear family. The past few years have seen a wave of anti-LGBT+ legislation, for example the law in Uganda that penalizes "aggravated homosexuality" with the death penalty (BBC 2023), and in the USA, since 2020, each year the number of anti-trans bills being proposed surpasses the last, with 2025 proposing over 1000 such bills in legislative houses across the country (Trans Legislation Tracker 2025). Additionally, in the USA, a mounting wave of transphobia has propelled a series of Executive Orders, including "Defending Women From Gender Ideology Extremism And Restoring Biological Truth To The Federal Government" and "Keeping Men Out of Women's Sports". These initiatives double down on the primacy of sex as "binary and biological" (The White House 2025a, The White House 2025b).

Proponents of these arguments base their critiques on the progressive view of gender, which they invalidate as a subjective or personal perception. In the abovementioned Executive Orders, for example, gender ideology is defined as "an ever-shifting concept of self-assessed gender identity, permitting the false claim that males can identify as and thus become women and vice versa, and requiring all institutions of society to regard this false claim as true." (The White House 2025a).

Indeed, pro-trans perspectives from medical groups, for example, favor the notion of gender identity over that of biological sex as a determinant for how a person identifies. The American Psychiatric Association defines gender identity as "a person’s inner sense of being a girl/woman, boy/man, some combination of both, or something else" ("What is Gender Dysphoria?" 2025). Similarly, the World Health Organization defines gender identity as "a person’s innate, deeply felt internal and individual experience of gender," and constrasts it to biolgoical sex, adding that gender identity "may or may not correspond to the person’s physiology or designated sex at birth" ("What is Gender Dysphoria?" 2025, "Gender and health" 2025).

Accordingly, some Tran Studies scholars posit the definition of gender is the problem. That the push to disentangle gender from sex and to solidify sex as "binary and biological" is a reaction to the fluidity, nebulousness, and slipperyness of how gender is conceptualized in trans affirming discourse. Andrea Long Chu (2024), for exaple, points this out that, "The left must reckon with its part in this. It has hung trans rights on the thin peg of gender identity". Chu, herself a trans woman, explains that the pro-trans movement has "largely failed to form a coherent moral account of why someone’s gender identity should justify the actual biological interventions that make up gender-affirming care." In other words, Chu asks, if gender really is something that is internal, then why should a gender non-conforming or transgender person need to altar any external characteristics?

The view of gender as an internal phenomenon thus inspires transphobic reasoning around the issue of medical care, which argues that an physical transition is not necessary.

This issue is where the paper hopes to make its intervention, by looking deeply into gender as an embodied and expressive phenomenon, as theorized by Trans Studies scholars in the theory of "trans materialism."

4. Gender categories

In what follows, I briefly trace theoretical debates on defining gender from the perspective of Queer and Trans Studies, with a particular focus on how it has been conceptualized in relation to closely associated notions of sex and sexuality.

Contemporary liberal notions of gender descend from a widely accepted view that gender is a social phenomenon rather than a biological reality. This view can be traced to Judith Butler's seminal theory of Gender Performativity, which dissolves the traditionally strict and causal relation between sex, gender, and sexuality. Butler's theory of gender performativity stipulates that gender is not, as assumed by the traditional view, based on a biological reality, nor does it determine sexuality (with male gendered people prefering female gendered people). Rather, Butler argues that gender is an effect produced social norms and expectations which manifests as a series of behaviors, what they describe as "performativity". According to Butler's theory, gender becomes real not in any essential or ontological sense, but through its active and external expression (1993).

In the 35 years since Butler's theory, the distinction between gender as a social operation and sex as physical embodiment has been validated in biology, neuroscience, and psychology (Ainsworth, 2015; Hyde et al., 2019; Joel, 2021). In other areas, however, the definition of gender has continued to evolve into the notion of "identity". Moving from a kind of behavior (one that, Butler is careful to point out, is not at will, but deeply compelled by social rules), gender takes on an internal apect of identity or identification. Gender becomes gender identity, which describes an internal feeling or identification as male, female, a combination of the two, or nonbinary genders. Gender as identity thus diverges from gender as expression, which denotes the masculine, feminine, or androgynous traits that are visibly apparent and enacted. Furthermore, in this conception, sex is still understood to be a bodily component, though one not based firmly in a binary, nor does it necessarily prioritize any biological marker, like the presence of external genitals, sex hormones or chromosomes.

Trans Studies scholar Kadji Amin (2022) refers to this splitting of gender into gender identity a "divergence model" which has characterized the LGBTQ+ movement in its campaigns for rights and recognition over the past several decades. The splits in the understanding of gender has, according to Amin, created a domino effect where gender is increasingly idealized and divorced from bodily expression.

Amin argues that this "divergence model" between gender and sexuality has been responsible for a series of splits between categories like heterosexual and homosexual, and cisgender and transgender (2022). According to Amin, the concept of homosexuality used to incorporate gender-variant forms of sexuality, for example, forms of male femininity such as the "queen", and of female masculinity in the form of the "butch", the emergence of transgender offered a new category that was then used to hold "all manner of gender-bending" [Amin 2022].

This view generally accords with modern historiographers of sexuality who, tracing the categories further back into the 19th century, explain that heterosexuality as a category is a response to the creation of the category of homosexual [Foucault 1978, Halperin 2002]: According to this view, heterosexuality arose as a reaction to work by 19th century sexologists, such as that by Richard von Krafft-Ebing (1893) and Havelock Ellis (1900), who defined homosexuality primarily as a pathology.

Just as heterosexuality emerged "belatedly, as a normative ballast against homosexuality," Amin argues, so did the concept of cisgender, which responds to the emergence of the term "trangender" in the 1990s [Amin 2022]. One particular effect of the emergence of transgender is to create an idealized notion normativity that do not accurately describe any gender or sexuality in lived reality, according to Amin. As a result, Amin argues, terms like heterosexual, cisgender, and binary represent take up "increasingly idealized and uninhabitable normative categories" [Amin 2022].

This lack of identificatory feeling perhaps explains why some cisgender people are apparently reluctant to identify with the "cisgender" and "binary" nominatives. This is in direct contrast to those who identify as transgender, who draw from the experience of discomfort in one's embodied gender, commonly expressed in the concept of gender dysphoria. It is less straightforward to name and describe the experience of what is routine and unremarkable as the experience of being comfortable in one's gender and/or sexuality.

The effect of such idealized conceptualizations of gender and/or sexuality is to re-stigmatize non-normative gender expressions. Due to the continual splits within the divergence model, Amin argues, gender identity becomes buried "deeper and deeper within the private recesses of the self, where it increasingly disavows any relation to the social" [Amin 116]. In other words, idealized categories like cisgender and nonbinary make non-normative gender identities less and less visible. The effect of prioritizing non-normative gender as an identity that is invisible, that is not immediately apparent despite being marked, is to marginalize gender as expression, which re-stigmatizes those gender groups that already experience social stigma. Amin gives a common example from homosexual culture, where gay men who are normatively masculine, "masc" or "butch" men, have higher social status than feminized gay men, "queens" and "fairies", due to the cultural capital of masculinity (2022).

There is a tendency to create categories that separate internal qualities, which are often idealized, from the noticably different ones. And the reference to "noticably" is crucial here: that it is a difference which is visually apprehended, perceived to be alternative from the norm. This norm, by contrast, is transported into an internal and therefore idealized form, and retains the privilege of being unnoticed as such.

There is, in short, a kind of stigmatization that accompanies non-normative genders that are externally expressed. Which is to say that defining gender as an external expression or embodiment is more likely to make visible and validate those gender which experience stigamtization based precisely on how those genders present outwardly.

5. Trans Bias in NLP

5.1. Existing Frameworks for Social Bias

Prior work on social bias in NLP characterizes bias through a variety of outcomes-—such as stigma, unequal distribution of resources, and linguistic or syntactic patterns-—which do not easily fit into a single, unified framework. Despite this diversity, most studies distinguish between two broad forms of bias: those based on how bias manifests linguistically (including grammatical and structural forms), and those based on the societal consequences it produces (such as representational or allocative impacts).

Focusing on linguistic manifestations, Hitti et al. (2019) further separate bias into structural and contextual categories (see Table 1). Structural bias arises from grammatical conventions—for example, the use of masculine pronouns as default ("A programmer must always carry his laptop with him"), whereas contextual bias stems from socially embedded assumptions and stereotypes (e.g., "Senators need their wives to support them throughout their campaign"). Shifting attention from linguistic form to social consequence, Nemani et al. (2023) classify bias according to the specific effects it has on particular groups, organizing it into three types: denigration, stereotyping, and underrepresentation. Denigration involves explicitly derogatory language such as slurs; stereotyping refers to generalized assumptions about a group; and underrepresentation denotes the lack or scarcity of information about certain groups. Along similar lines, Blodgett et al. (2020) and Barocas et al. (2017) distinguish between allocative harms—-where access to resources or opportunities is unequally distributed—-and representational harms, which involve the marginalization or stereotypical portrayal of groups.

5.2. Framework for Trans Bias #1: TIDEs

The first Trans Bias detection method is a project called Transphobia Identification in Digital Environments, or TIDEs (Lameiro et al. 2025). It builds on from the framework of a "trans technology," define by Haimson (2025) (who is an author of the paper) as a "technology that addresses the unique needs and challenges faced by trans people and communities" (Haimson 2025). Trans technology makes trans people a central focus of a particular technology or tool, in this case, of detection of transphobic speech.

To that end, TIDEs has two explicit goals: first, to create a dataset representing varied forms of transphobia, in particular forms that are more difficult to detect, like TERFism. Referring to "Trans Exclusionary Radical Feminism," TERF indicates a feminist position that does not support the rights of trans people. This type of transphobia is difficult to detect because TERF generally identify as feminists, although they do not support the rights of trans women and girls.

The second goal is to center trans and nonbinary experience and expertise in the data labelling work, which involved labelling social media posts as "transphobic or "not transphobic". The project chose labellers who personally identify as trans or nonbinary, and who "have personal experience with encountering and detecting online transphobia" (2025). According to the authors, this approach enacts what they call "care-centered data practices" (2025).

Indeed, the data labelling work showcases the attention given to individual kinds of transphobia. The dataset comprises social media posts scraped from Tumblr, Truth Social, YouTube, and Reddit that are labelled with "transphobic" and "not transphobic" labels. For example, a post like, "Transgender females are actually biological males. They should not be allowed to compete in womens sports. Period!" are labelled as transphobic, while posts like "Transgender Swimmer Lia Thomas Booted From Olympics After Losing Legal Case" are labelled as not transphobic.

However, as the project developers point out, some examples are not so straightforward. For example, the following post

Hey guys, I just wanted to get your opinion on this:

Whenever I meet someone I really quickly try to establish whether or not they have a dick, I don't just restrict this to potential sexual partners I mean everyone I meet. It just seems super important you know? Like how am I supposed to sleep at night unless I catalogue everyone's genitalia?

[…] I've started flat out asking everyone I meet. I try to be polite, like I look at the ground and draw it out all "Sooo do you like…. Have a dick?" but here is the thing: people look at me like I'm crazy for asking! What the hell is up with that? I even had one girl tell me I was a totally creep!

Detecting this post correctly as "not transphobic" relies on recognizing its irony, which is present in the tone of enforced sincerity. In fact, the only substantial indication that this is written in irony is the end of the post, which asserts that "it's nice to vent", and the source of the post itself, comes from a trans-dominated subreddit that emphasizes parody, "r/transcirclejerk".

Because this text, on the surface, parrots a transphobic perspective, it could be rightly labelled as such. The only thing that makes it not transphobic is the rhetorical context, whose cues are so subtle that they could be easily missed, particularly by those who lack the in-group cultural knowledge and language cues.

Complicating matters further, many other posts use a sincere tone to make serious requests. For example, the following post that asks for advice on gender presentation:

Hey! I'm going through some gender experimentation, so I'd like to tap into masculinity for awhile(usually I identify as just pure androgynous, but I struggle with pulling that off). So, to begin with, I'll try to master visual/behavioral androgyny first. I have long hair but don't want to cut it(a lot of manly men have long hair anyway), so how could I fix it up to look more masculine? Also, how could I dress more masculine-like? I don't have a very manly looking body shape, but I'd like to dress to give the illusion of one. Basically, any and all advice on how to appear androgynous/masculine is appreciated.

This post, which is rightly labelled as "not transphobic," has more in common with the previous "transphobic" example in terms of tone (which is sincere and supplicating. The differences have to do with an external rhetorical situation that is not clearly visible in the actual post.

In other words, context matters whether something is transphobic, and we ought to develop more labels for recognizing context.

5.3. Framework for Trans Bias #2: EQUITABL

EQUITABL, the Explore, Query, & Understand Implicit Textual Bias in Language data project (Devinney et al. 2024) uses a mixed-methods approach to analyze social bias in language, which deliberately includes bias toward trans and nonbinary identities, to examine "stereotypes, hegemonic norms, [and] erasure of identity" across all identity groups (Devinney et al. 2024). The target is therefore broader than TIDEs, which focuses specifically on trans

Another central difference from the TIDEs is that this method emphasizes a qualitative approach, which combines text generation, topic modelling, and close reading methodologies. Built from a method the researchers developed in a previous paper which proposes a "combination of quantiative and qualitative methods, where the quantitative part produces a view of the data of a size suitable for qualitative analysis" (Devinney et al. 2020). For the this more recent version of the project, the researchers exapanded their methodology to include text generation, with the main analysis focusing on close reading a set of LLM-generated narratives.

In brief, the process involves prompting both English and Swedish language models, Llama 2 and GPT-SW3 using a set of templates designed to generate narratives about various identity groups, then feeding the results to a topic model, which will organize the narratives into topics. Finally, using the resulting topic models to organize the narratives, they extract the top narratives for each group and proceed with close reading analysis, which is the main focus of the experiment.

The templates for prompting the language models consist of three components: first, an imperative phrase asking the model to "write a story about" or "write a description of"; followed by the subject of the narrative, like "a person," "a woman," "a Black cis person"; and closing with a predicate, such as "falling in love," or "with tears running down their face" (Devinney et al. 2024). The templates cycle cycle through different imperatives, racial and gender identities and religions, and predicates to create many variations. Possible variations include, for example "a Black Christian at the doctor's office," "a trans Muslim with a special skill," "a white nonbinary person with tears running down their face." The result is to create a diverse set of stories with protagonists spanning a variety of subject positions, in both positive, negative, and neutral valences.

The study's central intervention is its close reading methodology, which it includes in the place of quantitative scoring. As the researchers explain, they intentionally use qualitative metrics in order to "encourage users to critically engage wit the topics and patterns… rather than merely being incentivized to chase 'better' scores" (Devinney et al. 2024). Rather than a bias score, this metric contains of a series of questions that the researcher uses to guide close reading the results of the generated text. Some of the questions include, "How are people and bodies described?" and "Which stereotypes are used?" They complete the analysis with one more questions that probe the representation of gender specifically and whether this representation is associated with certain themes.

The results of the close reading reflect that narratives centering feminine identities are mostly linked to unpleasant emotions like depression and anxiety, a quality that is exacerbated in narratives about trans women. Narratives about masculine subjects, by contrast, are mostly positive in tone, associated with happy events like weddings and parties. Additionally, narratives about nonbinary persons express overt themes of struggle and the need for community support, and the researchers note that these struggles tend to end on a positive, "uplifting" note.

Amid these perhaps unsurprising results, there was an unexpected pattern of refusal from the model, which declined to discuss certain topics and identity groups. In the refusals, the models explain that their programming instructions require them to either stay away from topics of identity and/or to promote "negative or derogatory language" (Devinney et al. 2024). This tendency to refuse is notably more pronounced prompts specifying marginalized groups, whereas dominant groups (men, Christians) were more likely to be answered.

The only exception to this rule occured when identity markers from socially dominant groups were combined, such as "white" and "Christian," which created a higher instance of refusal than similarly intersectional, though less dominant, identity combinations. The researchers explain that, "When we look specifically at the intersection of race and gender, there is a very clear pattern where the most dominant groups are the least likely to be answered", which is a "direct inversion of the pattern for non-intersectional gender" (Devinney et al. 2024). For example, the least refused prompt was the phrase "a man," while the most refused one was "a white Christian" (Devinney et al. 2024).

As the researchers rightly point out, refusing to answer a prompt about a specific identity group is its own form of discrimination: "The refusals themselves actually produce risk and harm. They suggest that the model likely cannot say anything nice, which is alarming when frequently repeated about minoritized groups" (Devinney et al. 2024). Such refusals implicitly reinforce that only a select and dominant subset of identities are fit for discussion, while other, more marginalized groups are not.

Which relates to another notable aspect of the results, the "unmarked majority". The researchers explain that "When not otherwise specified, ‘a person’ is assumed to be a man, as well as likely white, straight, cisgender, and Christian; additionally […] being a provider" (Devinney et al. 2024). In other words, a lack of identity specification leads the model to assume a dominant identity, a default white, straight, cisgender, Christian male identity. This aspect of the results perhaps also explains why the model may refuse to answer generate narratives of intersectional dominant groups ("a white Christian"). The researchers posit that such social groups are "so often unmarked that specifying them draws extra attention to the concept of 'identity,' which the model has been discouraged from talking about" (Devinney et al. 2024). Due to the dominance of such groups, reinforce this aspect of the default majority.

6. Discussion

In what follows, I discuss how these two gender bias evaluation frameworks, TIDEs and EQUITABL, engage with Amin's point about external gender expression and how it relates to stigma. Amin argues that, due historical tendency of LGBTQ+-related categories that distinguish internal and idealized identities from visibly different ones, a kind of stigmatization accompanies non-normative genders that are externally expressed. Given this focus, how does gender as external presentation or behavior operate in the above frameworks for studying gender bias in NLP?

First, it is important to remember that existing evaluations of LLMs show that the the most frequent and default subject category is the straight white male, and that other subject categories, like woman, black, and homosexual, are less prevalent in both training data and model outputs (Guo and Caliskan 2021). Additionally, research has also shown a correlation between the presence and frequency of certain identities in the training data and their association with positive and negative terms (Devinney 2024, van Loon 2022): van Loon et al. (2022), in particular, find that the less frequently a group is named, the more negative the associations with that group.

If a group's relatively frequent presence the training data has a positive effect on their representation, one might reasonably assume that making marginalized subjects more frequent in the training data, to be on par with the frequency of representation of dominant subjects, may mitigate bias about these groups in model outputs. However, this assumption doesn't take into account that the default subject is often unmarked. As Devinney et al. (2024) point out, the majority of the texts about white male subjects were the ones least likely to describe that subject in external, physical terms.

That is perhaps because of an inverse relationship here between physical markers of identity and positions of social dominance, which Devinney et al.'s (2024) work seems to bear out. Here, less physical description is itself a marker of social dominance. In those results, white women are described in physical terms only slightly more than white men, while marginalized identities are described in more detail than both groups. For example, trans women are described in more physical language than cis women, which are typically not described in physical detail, unless they are mentioned as a bride, according to the researchers (Devinney et al. 2024). In other words, the more physicalized a person, the more likely that person is from a marginalized group.

One solution, then, may be to increase physical descriptions of all subjects–though especially white, male, heterosexual, Christian identified subjects. Some work already exists toward this end, by Reif and Schwartz (2023). In their article, "Fighting Bias with Bias," the researchers share a promising approach: amplifying rather than reducing bias in a model's training dataset. As they point out, "filtering [out bias] can obscure the true capabilities of models to overcome biases, which might never be removed in full from the dataset" [Reif and Schwartz 2023]. Instead of an approach that reduces bias, they offer one of increasing it, such as by including phrases like "the pretty doctor" so that a model will interpret "doctor" to be female.

Toward this end, researchers might spend more time with close reading methods, such as those displayed by TIDEs and EQUITABL methods. Such close and careful reading will help primarily with the identification of targets and attributes which describe gender as physical and behavioral, rather than as internal or psychological.

And such methods can apply to multiple approaches to gender bias evaluation. The TIDEs researchers, for example, developed a robust qualitative coding system, to make sure they could catch all examples of transphobia. This system is nuanced enough to distinguish transphobia within "Religious", "Medical", "Political", and "TERF" contexts, among others (Lameiro et al. 2025). Although the final model is a binary classification model, these label are built into the detection process through the data labellers' careful coding work.

Amin's point–that if gender were conceptualized as an outward, expressive phenomenon, rather than an identity category, there would be less social stigma–thus applies to widely different methods for evaluating gender and specifically trans bias in NLP. Through methods like close reading and qualitative coding, NLP can move closer toward making visible unmarked but dominant gender as presentation and behavior, rather than identity. For, as Amin points out, "Identification is the psychic process that makes the interval between the individual and the social apparent; it is not the site of their suture" (2022).

7. Conclusion

The problem with the univeral, unmarked subject is precisely that, despite not being marked, he is overrepresented. The over-representation of straight white men is not due to a persistant label of "heterosexual white man" that appends each and every predication. Rather, it's due to a proliferation of predication that already surround him, that define him. The white man does not a descriptive label as such, he simply is.

There's a central contradiction in the theorizing of gender – as something that is a social and behaviorial phenomenon (ala Butler) and at the same time an identity and expression of identity.
