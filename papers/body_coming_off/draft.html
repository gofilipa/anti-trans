1.1. cis and trans common ground in media

While this kind of critique looks for common ground between cis and trans bodily experience, the media representation of trans subjects is a separate issue. As scholarship in media studies have argued, trans studies subjects occupy distinct roles and thus require their own models of analysis. Thomas Billard and Erique Zhang explain that existing queer and feminist rubrics, which operate within (and against) contexts of patriarchy and heteronormativity, do not apply in the same way to the experience of trans subjects. They ask, for example, "Where does the trans man fall in the hierarchy of patriarchal domination? Does he now dominate women by virtue of his transition?" (Billard and Zhang 195). The authors argue that trans media subjects need their own frameworks for representation, one that is based on reading how trans people represent themselves.

That being said, the major argument of this paper is that the experience of cis subjects might be read under trans studies frameworks, albeit within certain constraints. While cis and trans media representations vastly contrast each other, with trans subjects occupying distinct roles and plots from cis ones, they nonetheless may overlap and intersect temporarily. For example, as Cáel M. Keegan points out, the line between cis and trans dissolves where gender norms are experienced as enforced and oppressive. He discusses the film The Assignment (2016), where a hitman named Frank is punished by being forceably transitioned into a woman by a rogue surgeon. Keegan argues that Frank evokes a plight of a trans man: "Frank is forced to live and behave as a woman because he has been assigned a female body… [the film] is arguably about the experience of transgender men—a point entirely missed by the press coverage" (Keegan 34). In Keegan's argument, such "bad objects" for trans media representation, which represent trans lives through tropes of suffering and stereotype, eventually lead to "a world in which the distinction between cis and trans ceases to exist altogether, because the systems enforcing binary sex and gender are dismantled" (36).

1.2. reality tv participants being radical due to audience

Reality TV scholars have long expressed reality tv participants' conscription into the merchandizing logics of the production. From the earliest shows, participants are organized into plots and formats that reinforce larger commercial interests and messages. While scenes are supposedly "unscripted," the settings (incluing particular formats like the confessional and the reunion) and personages (who must apply to be accepted on the shows) are meticulously chosen. As Yael Levy explains, "The tension between production interests, commercial interests, and the self-reflexive desires of participants positions the reality participant… in constant negotiation between narrative and performance, agency and commodification” (747).

That being said, there are moments where the participants do break through the confines of these logics. As Levy argues, reality TV creates openings or "textual creases" from which characters can find space for self-representation (Levy 747). One early, often cited example of this kind of break is Pedro Zamora, an out, Latino PWA (person with AIDS) from The Real World: San Francisco (1994). According to Jose Esteban Munoz, Zamora was able to publically represent the life of a gay and Latino PWA at a time when such subjects were hidden from public view. Due to the white and hetero-normative makeup of his audience, Zamora's on-screen representation "exploited MTV in politically efficacious ways; he used MTV more than it used him" (Munoz 152). To a majority queer, trans or nonwhite audience, Zamora's message would have been less effective. Munoz points out that "Zamora's work was not for other activists, queer or Latino, but was instead for a world of potentially politicized queers and Latinos; for a mass public that is structured by the cultural forces of homophobia and racism" (146).

This project also takes those contexts into account in judging the political efficacy of the show. The majority cishetero audience of Love is Blind creates a situation where experiences of disembodiment are potentially radical.

1.3. shake

When the show airs, Irina catches a lot of criticism from viewers for the way that she treated Zach, for what they interpret as her superficial rejection of Zach based on his looks. This emphasis on physical attraction in romance, which directly contradict the show's main gambit, that "love is blind," recalls another Love is Blind participant who, like Irina, is demonized for holding superficial notions around beauty. Shake, an Indian American, male character from season 2 of the show, cheekily declares that "love is blurry," in the show's reunion episode. His arrogance throughout the reunion, combined with his chauvanistic comments aimed at many of his female co-stars, paint him as the ideal villain. However, as Yael Levy argues, Shake is offensive and subversive at the same time: "[he] pokes at the show’s aspiration to prove that the body is a site of prejudice and that eliminating the body allows one to represent oneself beyond gender, race, or age bias" (Levy 758).

Similarly to Levy, I argue that Irina's attachment to visual attraction also belies the show's main gambit. However, this project traces Irina's rejection beyond typical social conceptions of beauty and into her specific experience in the pods, which fractures the sensory modes bewteen touch and sight.
